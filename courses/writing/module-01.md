# Module 1: The Sentence

**Stage I — The Sentence** · **Status: ✅ Complete** · 2026-07-25 → 2026-07-28 · **Verdict: PASS**

**Core question:** What makes one sentence land and another die on the page?

**What you walk away with:** the ability to build a sentence that carries weight, and to hear the rhythm of a paragraph well enough to fix it.

---

## Lecture (framing)

Here is the uncomfortable premise of this entire course: **there is no such thing as good writing made of bad sentences.** Structure, voice, reporting, argument — all of it is built on the sentence, and none of it survives if the sentences underneath are mush. This is why we start here, and why Stage I is not throughput on the way to the interesting material. It *is* the material.

Four things to hold in mind as you work through today's sources.

**1. The sentence is the unit of composition, not the paragraph.** Most people write in a smear — they aim at a general meaning and let words fall roughly around it. A professional writes one sentence, finishes it, checks that it is true and clear and complete, then writes the next. Klinkenborg's radical claim, which you'll read today, is that every sentence should stand alone and deliver its cargo without leaning on its neighbors. Test any paragraph you've written: pull a sentence out of the middle and read it by itself. If it collapses into meaninglessness, it was never doing its own work.

**2. Short is the default; long must earn it.** Nearly every writer who has thought hard about prose arrives in the same place: shorten. Not because short sentences are simple — because short sentences are *checkable*. You can see whether a nine-word sentence is true. A fifty-word sentence hides its failures inside its own clauses. Zinsser's version of this is "one thought per sentence," with the period as the tool that saves you. Klinkenborg's version is that a long sentence should still be a short sentence at heart — a clear spine with material hung on it, not a tangle.

You will resist this, and you should know why. School taught you that short sentences are childish and that sophistication reads as length and Latin. That training is the most expensive habit you have to unlearn. Read any paragraph of institutional prose — a press release, a bank's earnings language, an academic abstract — and notice that it sounds authoritative while communicating almost nothing. That effect is not an accident of bad writers. It is a *style*, it is available to you, and you must refuse it.

**3. The end of the sentence is the power position.** English loads emphasis at the end. "The trade lost money, unfortunately" and "Unfortunately, the trade lost money" carry identical information and land completely differently. Whatever you put last is what the reader carries forward. Most weak sentences are weak because their strongest word is buried mid-clause, cushioned by qualifiers on both sides. When you revise, ask of every sentence: *what is the last word, and is that the word I want ringing?*

**4. Rhythm is real, and it is controllable.** Prose has a beat. Five sentences of identical length produce a drone the reader stops hearing — the most common flaw in intelligent amateur writing, and invisible to the person who wrote it, because they were attending to content rather than sound. Vary lengths deliberately: a long sentence that gathers momentum, then a short one that stops it dead. That contrast is where emphasis comes from. Gary Provost's famous demonstration, below, makes this audible in about ninety seconds.

The practical instrument for all four: **read your work aloud.** Your ear catches what your eye forgives. Every professional does this. Most amateurs never do it once.

## Watch & Read

Two sessions, roughly half an hour each. Each has a check.

### Session A — The sentence as the unit (~25 min)

**1. Read: [Verlyn Klinkenborg on Writing More Clearly](https://lithub.com/verlyn-klinkenborg-on-writing-more-clearly/)** — *Literary Hub*, ~12 min. Klinkenborg taught writing at Yale for years and argues that nearly everything you were taught about writing is an obstacle to writing. Watch for: his three questions about every sentence — what it *says*, what it *omits*, what it *implies* — and his explanation of why writers resist short sentences.

**2. Read: [This Sentence Has Five Words — Gary Provost on varying sentence length](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2014/08/05/this-sentence-has-five-words/)** — Aerogramme Studio, ~3 min. The most efficient writing lesson ever composed: a passage that demonstrates its own argument as you read it. **Read it aloud.** That instruction is not optional — the passage only works through the ear.

## Discussion

Answer all three, then submit them together.

**1. The professional's disagreement.** Klinkenborg says each sentence should stand alone and not lean on its neighbors. Zinsser prizes sequential thinking — each sentence following logically from the one before. Are these in conflict? Take a position and defend it with an example.

**2. Where clarity is dangerous.** Institutional prose is vague on purpose: a Fed statement written in Anglo-Saxon monosyllables would move markets violently. Vagueness is sometimes a professional *skill*. As a writer whose beat is markets, when — if ever — is it legitimate for you to be less than maximally clear? ## Application

**The Rewrite Drill.** Harder than it sounds.

1. Take **300 words of your own published writing** — a Substack post is ideal, since it's already public and you can't hide behind "it was only a draft."
2. Rewrite under three constraints: **no sentence longer than 20 words**; **every sentence ends on the word you want emphasized**; **no Latinate abstraction where a concrete Anglo-Saxon word exists.**
3. Read both versions aloud. Note where the rewrite is better **and where it is worse** — this part matters. Constraints reveal things and also destroy things, and a professional knows which of their own habits were actually working.
4. Submit both versions plus a paragraph on what the constraints cost you.

### What happened

Perfect comprehension on both checks — the readings landed. The written work showed real movement: in the Rewrite Drill, a 17-word clotted sentence ("I started to notice that there was a pattern in the people with whom I had positive interactions") became "I noticed a pattern," and a two-word sentence — *No shit.* — was dropped in after a run of longer ones. That last move is Provost's rhythm lesson executed by instinct rather than by rule, and it's the strongest evidence in the submission that the module took.

Best analytic moment: in discussion Q1, catching the course's own prose in the act — noting that "Are these in conflict?" builds on its predecessor and asking whether that violates Klinkenborg. Correct observation, and the resolution was taught in response: reference is fine, *dependence* is the flaw. A sentence may point backward; it may not collapse without its neighbor.

Self-diagnosis in Session A stopped one step short. The third sentence of the sampled paragraph — "It does this through building an algorithm that is personalized for each person" — says the algorithm builds an algorithm. The error was invisible at twenty words and unmissable at six, which is Klinkenborg's thesis demonstrated on live material.

### Carried forward into Module 2

1. **Comma splices** — two instances across two submissions. Read every comma: if both sides are complete sentences, it wants a period.
2. **Cut abstractions, protect specifics.** In the rewrite, "40s-50s" was deleted while *nice*, *older*, *energetic*, and *connected* survived — the trade ran backwards. Numbers and physical details are the last things to cut, not the first.
3. **Interrogate your own drafts harder.** The reflection named what the constraints gained but not what they cost. The real answer was available: the rewrite dropped the mechanism (younger people weren't homeowners), which was the evidence for its own conclusion.
