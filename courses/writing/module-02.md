# Module 2: Diction & the Working Vocabulary

**Stage I — The Sentence** · **Status: ⏳ In progress — 3 items outstanding** · Graded 2026-07-29 · **Verdict: INCOMPLETE**

**Core question:** How do you find the exact word — and how do you come to own enough words that the exact one is available when you need it?

**What you walk away with:** a working method for choosing words precisely, and a permanent system ([The Lexicon](lexicon)) for converting words you merely recognize into words you actually use.

---

## Lecture (framing)

**The distinction that governs this module: recognition vocabulary versus working vocabulary.** You recognize somewhere between five and ten times more words than you deploy. Reading is what grows the first number. Only deliberate practice grows the second — and only the second one shows up in your prose. Every serious writer has a system for moving words across that border. This module builds yours.

Which means: **flashcards are the wrong tool, and "word of the day" is worse.** A word learned as a definition is a word you will misuse, because meaning lives in usage — in register, connotation, and the company a word keeps. *Slender*, *thin*, *skinny*, *gaunt*, *svelte*, and *emaciated* share a denotation and are not interchangeable in any sentence you will ever write. The only reliable way to acquire a word is to meet it repeatedly in the wild, notice it, record the sentence it lived in, and then deliberately use it three or four times until it's yours. That is what a **commonplace book** is — a practice running from Renaissance humanists through Milton, Locke, Emerson, and Didion, all of whom kept notebooks of other people's sentences.

**Three axes govern every word choice.**

*Concrete versus abstract.* "The firm experienced significant adverse developments" versus "the firm lost $400 million and fired 300 people." Abstraction is where meaning goes to hide. It's also where liars go. Your default should be the most concrete word available, and you should treat your own abstractions as suspects.

*Anglo-Saxon versus Latinate.* English is a hybrid: Germanic core, French and Latin superstructure imported after 1066. The Anglo-Saxon layer is short, concrete, and emotionally loaded — *house, child, bread, blood, break, die*. The Latinate layer is long, abstract, and institutional — *residence, offspring, sustenance, hemorrhage, fracture, decease*. Neither is better in the abstract. But the Latinate layer carries an odor of officialdom, and using it by reflex is how prose becomes bureaucratic without its author noticing. Choose it when you want distance or formality; notice when you're choosing it out of insecurity.

*Denotation versus connotation.* Denotation is the dictionary; connotation is everything the word drags in with it. *Regime* and *administration* denote roughly the same thing and imply completely different judgments. Half of what a professional writer does with word choice is managing connotation — and half of what a propagandist does is the same thing, which is exactly Orwell's subject today.

**Etymology is the cheat code for retention.** A word whose history you know is a word you rarely forget, because you've attached it to a story. *Sarcasm* comes from Greek for tearing flesh. *Companion* is Latin for "with bread" — the person you share bread with. *Salary* is salt-money. You'll use [etymonline.com](https://www.etymonline.com/) as a working tool from here on, not a curiosity.

Today's central text is Orwell, and it is the most consequential essay ever written about English prose — partly because of its rules, but mostly because of its argument: that vague language and dishonest thought feed each other, and that the loop runs both ways. Clean up the language and you make certain lies harder to tell. This is why a writer's diction is an ethical matter and not just an aesthetic one.

## The Lexicon — starting your permanent system

This module launches a practice that runs for the rest of the course and, ideally, the rest of your writing life. Full instructions live on **[The Lexicon](lexicon)** page. The short version:

1. **Capture** — whenever you meet a word or phrase in reading that you *recognize but wouldn't have produced*, record it **with the full sentence it appeared in**. Never the bare word.
2. **Interrogate** — look up its etymology; note its register and connotation; note a near-synonym it is not interchangeable with.
3. **Deploy** — use it in your own writing three times within two weeks. Only then is it yours.

Your first entries go in today, from Orwell and Didion. Ten entries minimum before Module 3.

## Application

**The Precision Exercise.**

1. Write **one paragraph (150 words max)** describing a single real moment you witnessed — a specific day, a specific event, what it actually felt like. Use no abstractions and no jargon whatsoever. Every noun concrete, every verb active. Difficulty: describe the feeling without naming the feeling.
2. Now write **the same paragraph in institutional English** — the version a corporate communications desk would publish. Latinate, passive, hedged.
3. In three sentences: what does version two *permit its author to avoid*, and when might a professional legitimately want that?
4. Submit all three parts, plus your first ten Lexicon entries.

### Carried forward

- **Stop hedging at the hard part.** "I really don't know" (M1 Q1) and "I don't really know" (M2 Q2) both arrived exactly where the question got interesting, and both times the answer was one step away. Plant a flag; being wrong in a draft is free.
- **Headwinds is weather** — the metaphor's real work is converting a management failure into an act of God. Ask of any dead metaphor: *taken literally, does it hold?*
- **Deleveraging usually means forced selling.** The word makes a margin call sound like a strategy.
