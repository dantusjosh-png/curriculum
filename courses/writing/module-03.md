# Module 3: Clarity & Clutter

**Stage I — The Sentence** · **Status: ⏳ In progress — 3 items outstanding** · Graded 2026-07-31 · **Verdict: INCOMPLETE**

**Core question:** Why does writing that is technically correct still fail to be understood?

**What you walk away with:** The ability to cut everything not doing work, and to escape the curse of your own expertise.

---

## Lecture (framing)

Module 1 was about the sentence as a unit. Module 2 was about the word inside it. This module is about a failure mode that survives both of those being right: prose that is grammatical, uses precise words, and *still* doesn't land — because the writer forgot what the reader doesn't know.

**1. The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation for bad writing by smart people.** Once you know something, you cannot easily simulate not knowing it. An expert explaining their field reflexively skips the steps that got automatic for them years ago — and has no internal alarm telling them a step is missing, because from inside their own head, nothing is missing. This is not a character flaw. It gets *worse* the more competent you become, which is why the driest, most impenetrable prose in the world is usually written by people who understand their subject perfectly. Pinker's case, which you'll watch today, is that this single mechanism explains more bad writing than ignorance, laziness, or bad grammar combined.

**2. The remedy is not "try harder to be clear" — it's structural.** You cannot self-correct the curse of knowledge by force of will, because the whole problem is that you can't see what you're missing from inside your own head. The actual fixes are procedural: hand the draft to someone who doesn't already know the material and watch where they stumble; or put it in a drawer and reread it cold after enough time has passed that you've forgotten your own shortcuts. Both work by manufacturing the reader's ignorance artificially, since you can't access it directly.

**3. Clutter and unclearness are not the same failure, and conflating them wastes your revision time.** A sentence can be short and still be unclear — an underspecified pronoun, a term of art dropped with no anchor, a causal leap the reader can't follow. A sentence can also be long and perfectly clear — clarity comes from structure and sequencing, not word count. Module 1 taught you to prefer short; this module teaches you the sharper test underneath that preference: **does removing this cost the reader anything?** If not, it's clutter, regardless of length. If it does, it stays, regardless of length.

**4. Clutter is usually the residue of the writer's own process, not the reader's needs.** Throat-clearing ("It is important to note that..."), hedging that protects the writer rather than qualifying the claim ("in some sense, one might argue..."), and scene-setting before the actual point — these earn their place in a first draft, where you're finding out what you think. They have no business surviving into a piece meant to be read. Roy Peter Clark's toolkit, today's second source, is full of instruments for finding and cutting exactly this kind of residue — most usefully **"cut big, then small": remove whole sentences and paragraphs before you tighten individual words**, because no amount of word-level polish rescues a paragraph that shouldn't exist.

The through-line: writing for a reader who knows less than you do is not a courtesy. It is the actual skill, and everything else in this module is a technique for doing it on purpose instead of by accident.

But writing for total beginners on every subject would make it impossible to write for a knowledgeable audience at all — some assumed knowledge is necessary, not a failure. ## Application

**The Explainer.**

1. Write a **400-word explainer** of one concept from journalism, travel, or anything else you know well — written for a smart reader who knows *nothing* about it.
2. No jargon without a definition attached in the same sentence you use it. No step skipped because it feels obvious to you.
3. In two sentences: which part was hardest to make legible, and what did writing it reveal about knowledge you didn't realize you were assuming?
4. Submit the explainer plus the reflection.

### Carried forward

- **The hedge-before-the-real-answer pattern has now shown up in every module.** M1 Q1 stopped at "I really don't know," M2 Q2 stopped at "I don't really know," M3 Q2 stops at "I don't know exactly why." Same move each time: reach the interesting part of the question, then set it down. Worth noticing before it's written, not after.
- **Tightening ≠ rewriting.** A word-level pass should not change what a sentence means. If a cut changes the content, it belongs in the "big cut" conversation, where it has to be justified out loud — not smuggled into what's framed as a small one.
