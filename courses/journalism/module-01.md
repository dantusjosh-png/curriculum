# Module 1: Finding the Story

**Status: ⏳ In progress** · Opened 2026-07-31

**Core question:** Where do stories actually come from, and how do you build
the network of people who'll bring them to you before anyone else has them?

**What you walk away with:** a working framework for judging whether something
is actually a story (not just an event), and the first concrete habits for
turning strangers into sources who trust you with things they haven't told
anyone else.

---

## Lecture (framing)

**News judgment is not a personality trait — it's a habit built from two
questions, asked of everything you notice: is it important, and is it
interesting?** Neither alone is enough. A city budget vote is important and
almost never interesting on its own; a squirrel stealing a sandwich is
interesting and almost never important. The stories worth chasing sit where
both are true at once, and the skill is noticing the overlap in things other
people walk past — what Roy Peter Clark calls recognizing that "an expert is
paying attention" where a non-expert sees nothing at all.

That noticing doesn't happen in a vacuum. It happens because you've built a
**network** — people who call you before the press release goes out, who
mention something in passing that turns out to be the whole story. Cultivating
that network is a completely different skill from writing, and it's the one
this module is actually about. It runs on ordinary human trust-building, not
tricks: showing sustained interest in people as people, not as information
sources; keeping your word about what's off the record, every single time,
because the first broken promise is also the last useful source; and treating
a good interview as the start of a relationship rather than a completed
transaction.

Today's two readings work as a pair: one gives you the framework for judging
*what's worth pursuing*, the other gives you the concrete habits for
*building the people who'll bring it to you*.

## Discussion

**1. Whose judgment?** Clark frames news judgment as a skill — an expert "paying attention" where others don't. But every judgment about what's important and interesting is made by someone, from some vantage point. Is "important and interesting" ever a neutral test, or does every exercise of news judgment quietly encode whose interests count as newsworthy in the first place? Take a position and defend it with an example.

<div class="cc-answer" data-id="journalism-m1-d1" data-group="journalism-m1-discussion" data-title="Q1 — Whose judgment?"></div>

**2. Against the pyramid.** The pyramid puts ethics and mission at the very top, as a culmination built on everything below it. Make the opposite case: that ethics has to be the *foundation*, not the summit, because a reporter who lacks it can't be trusted at any other level regardless of how skilled they are — a great interviewer with no ethics is more dangerous, not less. Which structure is actually right, and why?

Your own sourcing instinct.** Of the five tips in the cultivating-sources piece, which do you already do naturally without thinking about it, and which would feel the most foreign or uncomfortable to actually do? Be specific about *why* the uncomfortable one is uncomfortable — that discomfort is usually pointing at something real about how you relate to people.

## Application

**The Real Source Exercise.**

1. Identify one real person you could actually approach about something you're genuinely curious about — not a hypothetical subject, an actual person you have some path to reaching.
2. In two or three sentences, make the case that this clears *both* of Clark's bars — important and interesting — for a reader who has no personal connection to it.
3. Write the actual opening line of small talk you'd use with this person (per tip #1) — something real, not generic.
4. Write the first three questions you'd actually ask them, in order.
